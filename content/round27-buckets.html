I · The split

An income enters as one ink bar across the top and physically divides into four coloured streams that pour down into four vessels, each filling to its real level. The ribbon widths are the real shares, so the picture and the arithmetic are the same object.

This is the only idea that shows the act of dividing rather than the result of it — which is the thing the product does, and the thing four coloured panels never managed to say.

The cost: a Sankey is a chart, and charts are read rather than felt. It is also the most complex thing to build responsively — those ribbons are computed geometry, so a phone needs its own set rather than a squeeze of these.

A month has a shape.

Thirty days, every one of them drawn to what actually left. Rent on the 5th. SIPs on the 1st and the 8th. The small pink evenings that add up to more than you would guess.

II · The month

Not a summary — a portrait. Thirty day-cells, each drawn to what left that day and coloured by the bucket it left for. The 5th is a wall of amber because rent is a wall of amber. The weekends are a low pink rhythm.

It is the only one of the three that shows you something you cannot get from a total, and it is the most obviously "this is my life" of anything on the page. It also sits on ink, so it belongs to the hero's world rather than interrupting it.

The cost: thirty cells is a lot of small things after a hero that is already a grid of small things — the two could read as the same trick twice. And it needs a real month of data to stay honest; invented days would be the one dishonest thing on the page.

The whole month, on one roll.

Every rupee that left, printed in order, tabbed by bucket. Nothing summarised, nothing hidden.

III · The receipt

The section becomes one printed object: a paper roll down the middle of the viewport, mono, dashed rules, a perforated tear at the foot, every entry tabbed with its bucket colour. It prints itself line by line.

It is the most tactile thing the page could contain and the only one that feels like an artefact rather than a graphic — and a receipt is the one financial object everybody already knows how to read, so it needs no explaining at all.

The cost: a receipt is a record of the past, and the product's argument is about what is left. It is also the quietest of the three — a narrow column on cream after a full-bleed ink hero may read as the page losing its nerve.

My pick

II · The month. Three attempts at this section have failed the same way: they all restated the four names in a bigger font. A calendar of real days is the first version that puts something on screen you cannot get from the headline — and "the 5th is a wall of amber" is the kind of detail that makes a stranger believe the product is real.

I is the one to pick if the section should teach the mechanic; it is the clearest picture of what the product literally does. III is the one to pick if you want the page to feel handmade.
